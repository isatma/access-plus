Power wheelchairs

Driving and controlling the chair with inputs other than a hand joystick.

Drive system · electronicsCommercial

R-Net control system

The electronics platform inside many power wheelchairs, made by Curtiss-Wright and used by Permobil and other manufacturers. The joystick, power module, seating and add-on modules plug into one network, so new input modules can be added without replacing the chair.

  • Other chairs use different systems, such as Invacare's LiNX or Quantum's Q-Logic
  • Which system your chair has decides which input modules fit it
Interface · specialty input moduleCommercial

Omni2 (Permobil / R-Net)

A specialty input display that lets alternative controls drive an R-Net chair: head arrays, sip-and-puff, mini joysticks and switches. It also has built-in Bluetooth and infrared, so the same input can operate a computer, phone, TV and other devices.

  • Manufacturer-supported, so the chair's own safety system stays in charge
  • One input can drive, run seating functions and reach other devices

Interface · input moduleCommercial

R-Net Input/Output Module

A simpler R-Net module that accepts switch or proportional inputs, such as mini joysticks, for driving and functions when a full specialty display isn't needed.

Inputs · alternate drive controls

Ways to drive without a hand joystick

  • Head array: switches or sensors in the headrest; press back, left or right
  • Sip-and-puff drive: hard and soft sips and puffs choose direction
  • Mini and chin joysticks: very light-touch proportional control
  • Switch driving: one or more switches, momentary or latched
  • Proportional head control: head movement measured continuously
Beyond driving

What else the chair can control

  • Seating: tilt, recline, leg rests, seat height
  • Mouse and keyboard on a computer or tablet over Bluetooth
  • TV and other devices by infrared
  • Mounts for phones, screens and switches
Practice first

Try it in the simulator

Practice driving with a head array, sip-and-puff, switches or a mini joystick on obstacle courses before you try a real chair, and bring your scores to your seating clinic.

Wheelchair simulatorAsk the AI agent about drive controls
Access+ pilot work

Three university student teams are building new wheelchair inputs on a donated Permobil M3 Corpus with R-Net: an arm-crank exercise drive, a rowing pull trainer, and an upper-arm movement controller. The Omni specialty input module is under evaluation as the connection, so the chair's own fail-safe stays in charge.

Safety first: anything that drives a real wheelchair should be set up and tested with your seating specialist or supplier. Access+ sensor kits never connect to a wheelchair; driving practice happens in the simulator.

Wheelchair-mounted armCommercial

Kinova JACO assistive robotic arm

What it does
A lightweight six-axis arm with a three-finger hand, mounted on a power wheelchair. Used for eating, drinking, opening doors, picking things up, hygiene and work tasks.
How you control it
Through the wheelchair's own input: hand or mini joystick, head array, sip-and-puff, or switches, alone or combined.
Cost
Not published. Quoted per person through dealers after an evaluation; reported in the tens of thousands of dollars.
How it's funded
Varies widely. California Medi-Cal covers it as medical equipment since September 2024. Medicare has no policy for robotic arms, and some private and Medicaid plans list it as not covered. Veterans programs and charities fund some arms.
Who uses it
People with spinal cord injury, ALS, muscular dystrophy, SMA, cerebral palsy and upper-limb loss who drive a power wheelchair.
Skills you'd need
Safe, steady control of a power wheelchair input; switching between arm, wrist and hand modes; planning movements in 3D; judging distance and speed near your face.
Safety note
2024 FDA safety correction for a coating that can be damaged; owners were told to inspect the arm and contact Kinova for protective pads.

What's comingResearch

Smarter wheelchair and arm together

RAMMP, a five-year, up-to-$41 million ARPA-H project led by the University of Pittsburgh with Kinova, Carnegie Mellon, Cornell and others, is building a power wheelchair and arm that work as one system, with AI that can take over parts of a task, like opening a door, to reduce fatigue.

Project announcement ↗
How they help

What owners use them for

  • Eating and drinking on your own schedule
  • Opening doors, pressing elevator buttons, picking up dropped items
  • Scratching an itch, wiping your face, hygiene
  • Work, school and hobbies without waiting for help
  • Less caregiver time for small tasks, and more privacy
Why so few people have one

The barriers

  • Cost and funding: high price, no Medicare policy, and inconsistent insurance coverage
  • Proving ability: funders want evidence a person can use one safely, but it's hard to try one first
  • Fit: it has to mount on, and be powered by, a compatible wheelchair
  • Training: few clinicians have hands-on experience
  • Awareness: many people and therapists don't know arms exist
Getting one

The usual path

  1. Practice the control skills, for example in the Access+ simulator
  2. Ask your OT or seating clinic for an evaluation
  3. A trial with a dealer or the manufacturer
  4. Prescription and a letter of medical necessity
  5. Funding request; appeal if denied
  6. Mounting, setup and training

MotionInput

Camera-based hands-free control from a University College London spinout.

Windows software · webcamCommercialResearch

MotionInput (MotionInput Games Ltd., UCL Computer Science spinout)

What it does
Turns an ordinary webcam and microphone into a controller: nose, eye and face tracking, hand and body gestures, and speech, in 24 AI features that run on your own computer.
How you control it
Whatever movement you have: nose, eyes, blinks, facial expressions, hands, elbows, knees, leaning, or voice, including speech affected by dysarthria.
What you need
A Windows 10 or 11 PC and a webcam. Game controller support uses ViGEmBus; the app also needs Microsoft .NET.
Cost
Not listed on the features page. Check the MotionInput site.
Who uses it
People with limited hand use who want to run a computer or play games hands-free, plus exercise and rehabilitation uses.
Skills you'd need
Steady head or hand movement within the camera's view, picking which features to switch on, and calibrating in your usual seat or bed position.
Disclosure
A member of the Access+ team advises MotionInput Games.
Similar options
Free alternatives include Google's Project Gameface, Camera Mouse, Enable Viacam, and Apple's built-in Head Pointer. Compare them on the Head and face tracking page.

Neuralink (Telepathy / N1)

Where it's at
21 participants announced in January 2026, two years after the first implant (January 2024), in trials in the U.S. (PRIME), Canada, the U.K. and the UAE. Related studies for controlling a robotic arm and for speech are planned or underway. Blindsight, for vision, has an FDA breakthrough designation.
What people do with it
Move a computer cursor and click by thought, type, browse, play games and use design software.
How it works
A coin-sized wireless implant with very thin electrode threads, placed by a surgical robot.
Can I get one?
Only by joining a trial. People with quadriplegia from spinal cord injury or ALS can apply through Neuralink's patient registry.
Neuralink update ↗
Implant · through a blood vessel, no open brain surgeryResearch

Synchron (Stentrode)

Where it's at
Finished its U.S. COMMAND early feasibility study and is preparing a larger pivotal trial, the step before FDA approval.
What people do with it
Text, email, shop and use smart home controls with thought "clicks," combined with eye tracking. Users have controlled iPhone and iPad through Apple's BCI support.
How it works
A stent-like sensor is threaded up through a vein in the neck to sit beside the motor cortex.
Implant · arraysCommercialResearch

Blackrock Neurotech

Makes the Utah array used in most academic BCI research, including BrainGate, where people have typed, moved robotic arms and spoken through a computer voice. U.S. permanent use is still investigational.

Implant · thin film on the brain surfaceResearch

Precision Neuroscience

Its Layer 7 film received FDA 510(k) clearance in April 2025 for temporary recording, up to 30 days, in the hospital. Long-term implant use is still in trials.

Implant · speechResearch

Paradromics (Connexus)

Focused on restoring speech. FDA approved its clinical trial in late 2025, and recruitment began in 2026.

No surgery · headsetsCommercialResearch

Non-invasive EEG

Caps and headsets that read brain activity through the scalp. Far slower and less precise than implants, but no surgery, and some are used today for simple switch-style selections and research.

Why it matters

Who it could help

  • People with no reliable movement left, such as late-stage ALS or locked-in syndrome
  • High spinal cord injury where head, breath and eye control are tiring or limited
  • Restoring speech for people who can no longer talk
What to weigh

Honest trade-offs today

  • Brain surgery and its risks, for most implants
  • Trial access only, with strict eligibility and many visits
  • Long-term support if a company changes direction
  • Head, nose, eye and switch methods remain faster to start and fully reversible

Microphones

Choosing a microphone

  • Headset with a boom mic: most accurate because the mic stays the same distance from your mouth; noise-cancelling models block room sound
  • Wireless headset: freedom from cables in a wheelchair or bed; check battery life and pairing
  • Desk or array mic: nothing to wear, but needs a quiet room and steady position
  • Mounted mic: a small mic on a gooseneck on the wheelchair or bed rail, for people who can't put on a headset
  • Dragon publishes a list of microphones it has tested
Good to know

When voice works best, and when it doesn't

  • Great for writing, searching and commands
  • Hard in shared rooms, on the phone, or when breathing or voice tires
  • Pair it with head, nose or eye tracking for pointing, and a switch or facial gesture to mute
Practice

Build the skill

Start with ten core commands, add more each week, and keep a list of the commands you use most.

Eye tracking

Controlling technology with where you look. Often the method that keeps working as other movement changes.

Hardware and software · researchOpen sourceResearch

Low-cost DIY eye-tracking glasses (Augusta University)

Dr. Jason Orlosky, an associate professor in Augusta University's School of Computer and Cyber Sciences, has published an open-source eye tracker that runs on a pair of inexpensive glasses. A small infrared camera, around $17, clips to the frame and watches one eye. Free Python software finds the pupil and works out where the eye is pointing in 3D.

Hardware
Ordinary glasses or sunglasses with a small infrared eye camera attached; no special headset
Software
One Python script using OpenCV; shows gaze direction live and can save it to a file
What it can do
Gaze direction can work as a control input, for example looking at a menu item and blinking to select it
Background
Builds on a 3D eye-tracking system Orlosky and colleagues released in 2017; his lab works on augmented and virtual reality, eye tracking and AI
License
MIT (free to use, change and share)
Why it matters
Commercial eye trackers can cost thousands of dollars. A low-cost, open design lets people try eye control early, keep a spare, and lets volunteer makers and researchers adapt it

Two kinds of eye tracker

On the screen or on your face

  • Screen-mounted: a bar under the monitor or tablet; the usual choice for speech devices and computer access Proprietary
  • Head-worn glasses: the camera moves with you, so it can work in bed, in the chair, or away from a screen
Things to know

What to expect

  • Calibration matters, and needs redoing when you change position
  • Bright sunlight and some glasses can confuse the camera
  • Selecting usually means dwelling (holding your gaze) or a blink or switch
  • DIY builds may need a helper or maker group to assemble and set up

Hardware · mouse alternative · lip or chin

USB touchpad, used with the lip or chin

Any small external USB or Bluetooth touchpad, sold by many makers. A low-cost, do-it-yourself setup.
CommercialLow costNo extra software

An ordinary touchpad, mounted on an arm near the face, becomes a precise mouse. Sliding the lower lip or chin across it moves the pointer, and a tap clicks. It works with any computer that accepts a regular touchpad, with no special software.

Works with
Windows, Mac, Chromebook and most tablets that accept a mouse; pairs well with an on-screen keyboard such as OptiKey
Clicking
A tap on the pad, a dwell click in the computer's accessibility settings, or a separate switch
Mounting
A flexible gooseneck or articulating arm on the wheelchair, bed rail or desk, angled to the lip or chin
Settings
Pointer speed, tap-to-click on or off, palm rejection, and scrolling gestures, set in the computer's touchpad settings
Cost
Inexpensive compared with most assistive pointing devices; prices vary by model
Good for
People with reliable lip or chin movement who want fine pointing control; a useful backup to head or eye tracking

General guidance, November 2026. Try settings with your therapist and adjust for comfort.

Tips
  • Put a thin cover or film on the pad and wipe it regularly; it is personal equipment and is not shared
  • Start with slow pointer speed and turn up as control improves
  • Turn off tap-to-click if you get accidental clicks, and use dwell or a switch instead
  • Check lips and skin for irritation, and change position during long sessions
  • Practice pointing in the simulators to tune speed and target size

Wheelchair bridges

Connecting adaptive controls to a power wheelchair, and the chair's controls to everything else.

Into the chairA specialty input module lets head arrays, sip-and-puff, mini joysticks and switches drive the chair and run its seating.
Out of the chairA Bluetooth or infrared module lets the same controls work a computer, tablet, phone or TV, for example as a mouse or as switches.
Standard controlsComputers, phones and consoles understand keyboards, mice and gamepads (a standard called HID). A bridge translates an adaptive control into one of those.
Hardware bridge · into and out of the chair · R-net

Omni2 specialty input display

Made by Curtiss-Wright (PG Drives Technology) for R-net chairs, and sold by chair makers such as Permobil and Quickie. Replaces the original Omni, which is no longer made.
ProprietaryPossible Access+ loan item

Why it's used: it is the interface R-net chairs need for alternative drive controls. A person who can't use a hand joystick plugs in a head array, sip-and-puff, chin or mini joystick, or switches, and drives the chair, runs the seating, and reaches a phone, computer or TV, all from one input.

Compatibility
Power chairs with R-net electronics (used by Permobil, Quickie and other makers). Chairs with Invacare LiNX or Quantum Q-Logic electronics need their makers' own interfaces
Display section
3.5-inch colour screen with light sensor; buttons for on and off, profile, mode, settings and navigation; speaker and horn
Input section
R-net connector; two 9-way D-type sockets for specialty input devices; two 3.5 mm user-switch jacks; a sip-and-puff input; an optional external on/off jack; USB charging
Connects to
Up to two input devices at once, such as joysticks, switch panels, switched or proportional head controls, sip-and-puff and scanners
Out of the chair
Bluetooth to up to four devices (two as a mouse, two for iPhone and iPad Switch Control); learns infrared codes for a TV and other devices
How it's used
The user switch changes profiles and modes (drive, seating, Bluetooth, infrared) and doubles as an emergency stop. Drive profiles set speed and response for indoors, outdoors or tired days
To lend one
The chair must have R-net electronics; you also need the display, the input section, the connecting cable, a mount and the user's input device. Setup and programming are done by the seating provider

Details from Curtiss-Wright, Permobil and the Omni2 manual, checked November 2026. Confirm compatibility for a specific chair with the chair maker or dealer.

Installation, in brief
  1. Assess the user and choose the input device
  2. Mount the input section with its connectors facing down, to keep out moisture
  3. Mount the display on a flexible arm, such as a gooseneck
  4. Connect it to the chair's R-net system and plug in the input device
  5. Match the Omni2 to the input device, then tune it to the user
  6. Set the chair's driving characteristics and test
Safety
  • Programming is done on the display or by PC, only by professionals trained on R-net systems
  • Use a user switch that stops the chair if it is unplugged, and keep switch detection on, so the emergency stop always works
  • Don't drive if the screen is damaged or not working

Out of the chair · gamingOpen source

Wheelchair Bluetooth Adapter

A Raspberry Pi project from Makers Making Change (Neil Squire Society) that turns a chair's Bluetooth mouse output into a USB gamepad joystick, so you can play with the joystick you drive with. Works with Q-Logic, LiNX and R-Net chairs that have Bluetooth; tested on Windows and with the Xbox Adaptive Controller. Software under the MIT license.

Joytojoy

A 3D-printed box that fits over the wheelchair joystick knob. A motion sensor reads the joystick and sends it over USB as a mouse or a game stick. Versions for R-Net and LiNX joystick shafts; design files and code are shared publicly.

Choosing a bridge

Questions to ask

  • Which electronics does the chair use (R-Net, LiNX, Q-Logic)? That decides which modules fit
  • Which direction: driving the chair, or using the chair's controls for something else?
  • Is it supported by the chair maker, or a separate add-on?
  • Can a volunteer maker group build or adapt an open-source version?

Anything that connects to how a real wheelchair drives should be set up by your seating specialist or supplier. Add-ons that only read the chair's Bluetooth output don't change how it drives.
